===== NOTES 2 =====
Dominion's 2025 annual report gives SAIDI, excluding major events, of 133 minutes for the three years ending 2025, up from 130, and says: "This increase is primarily due to increased storm activity." SAIDI is average outage minutes per customer. I rebuilt it from EIA's filings. The public Virginia data reproduces both reported figures to the minute: 130.2 and 133.0, a rise of 2.8 minutes. A three-year average moves when a year leaves the window: 2025, at 144.4 minutes, replaced 2022, at 136.0. Between those two years storm county-days rose 10 percent, 791 against 718, and interruptions per customer, SAIFI, rose 10 percent; minutes per interruption, CAIDI, fell 4 percent. The weather fit in A2 puts 2025 on the storm line, and once storms are counted there's no clear trend. Two cautions: 2025's SAIFI of 1.320 only edges 2018's 1.303, and 2025's SAIDI is still below 2018 and 2019. One year is not a trend.

===== NOTES 3 =====
Is that unusual? These are twelve large investor-owned utilities in PJM and the Southeast, Dominion and eleven peers, picked by EIA ID before I looked at any results. Across is interruptions per customer per year; up is minutes per interruption; both are 2021 to 2025 averages. Dominion is the blue dot. Its minutes per interruption, 110, are sixth of twelve: mid-pack. Its frequency, 1.21 interruptions a year, is fourth highest, against a median of 0.93 for the eleven peers. One caveat I'd raise myself: utilities set aside major event days in different ways. Most of these peers use the IEEE 1366 method and Dominion reports under its own rule, so the ranking is indicative. The gap to peers is how often customers lose power.

===== NOTES 4 =====
Weather is the obvious question, and the county-day data answers it more finely than annual totals. EAGLE-I, the Department of Energy's county outage record, gives the customers out every fifteen minutes in each county. On county-days with a NOAA storm report, outage customer-hours per thousand customers averaged about 333, against about 28 on calm days: about twelve times, 11.9, with a 95 percent interval of 7.9 to 16.9, resampling whole days. It holds on non-major days, 9.9 times; in the counties Dominion serves alone, 19.9; and in every year from 2015 to 2025, between 5.9 and 27.4 times. Two cautions: EAGLE-I counts every utility's customers in a county, so this is not Dominion's SAIDI; and storm reports partly follow damage, so it's an association, not a cause. The year-level fit and the largest events are in the appendix.

===== NOTES 5 =====
The role's first listed duty is Power BI, so here is the report. Each page answers one question: the scorecard, what moved, weather, the fair comparison, outages by county, storms, growth and demand, and it ends on a data notes page with the 31 tests and the twelve reconciled values. The model is a star: one fact table at utility, state and year, many-to-one to a utility dimension for names and peer groups. Measures are customer-weighted: SAIDI is total customer-minutes over total customers, and the KPI cards follow the year slicer through Selected SAIDI. The report imports eighteen tables from the mart's CSV exports through one folder parameter, so it opens with no network or running warehouse.

===== NOTES 6 =====
Briefly, how it's built. Four public sources: EIA's reliability filings, NOAA Storm Events, the Department of Energy's EAGLE-I county outages, and EIA-930 hourly demand. They land in Snowflake as raw text exactly as published, a staging layer types and cleans them, and a mart of facts, dimensions and views feeds the Power BI report. Thirty-one tests pass: twelve on the original build, six of them on reliability, three on storms and three on demand, and nineteen on the new tables. The reliability tests check for duplicate keys, that SAIDI equals SAIFI times CAIDI, and that the 2025 values match what EIA published. A data dictionary covers every table and column, and the measures are defined in the metrics guide. Decisions are logged, D1–D22; the peer set was chosen by EIA ID before any results. The first check was reconciliation: twelve of twelve rebuilt values match the published figures, which confirms the definitions and filters. A local script, scripts/run_local_duckdb.py, rebuilds the core mart and runs its checks.

===== NOTES 8 =====
Three takeaways. One: 2025 was a stormier year, with more storms and more interruptions, and once storms are counted there's no clear underlying trend. Two: frequency is where Dominion differs from its peers, so the useful question is what causes the interruptions, and that needs outage records by cause; the ranks are indicative. Three: storm county-days carry far more outage hours than calm ones, so I'd report reliability beside storm exposure by county. On fit: at Mercury Insurance I built a Snowflake pipeline across policy, claims and billing data, over 20 million rows, and led a root-cause analysis on joined data. I've built a tested Snowflake to Power BI pipeline on public reliability data, and I'd like to do this work with your team. Thank you; I'm happy to take questions.

===== NOTES 9 =====
Definitions for reference. SAIDI equals SAIFI times CAIDI, which is why a change in SAIDI splits exactly into a frequency part and a duration part using logs. The split depends on the base year: from 2022, minutes per interruption fell, so frequency explains more than the whole rise.

===== NOTES 10 =====
Ten annual points, so the fit is a check, not a forecast. r is 0.70, but with ten points its 95 percent interval runs from about 0.12 to 0.92, weak to very strong, so on its own this is thin evidence; the county-day comparison on slide 4 rests on 6,108 storm county-days with complete outage readings. The table's storm county-days count all 103 Virginia counties Dominion serves. The fit starts in 2015 because the change to automated outage recording that year shifts the level. 2025 was left out of the fit and lands within a minute of it; the 95 percent prediction interval for a single new year was about 122 to 168 minutes. After the storm-day adjustment, the trend since 2015 is about plus 0.3 minutes a year, with a range that includes zero.

===== NOTES 11 =====
EIA's hourly demand for the PJM Dominion zone, which is wider than Dominion's retail territory. From 2019 to 2025 the hours ending 1 to 5 a.m. grew 39 to 41 percent, the hours ending 1 to 5 p.m. about 27. Holding Virginia's heating and cooling degree days and the weekday fixed, average load still rose about 33 percent, against 32 unadjusted. Winter set the yearly peak in 2019, 2022 and 2025, and 2026 so far is a tie. The two winter events in A6 took about 100 hours to clear 90 percent of the outage, against 20 to 66 for the other eight: two events, so a pointer, not a finding. This data can't identify customers, so I don't attribute the growth to data centers.

===== NOTES 14 =====
These are EAGLE-I events: every utility's customers in the counties Dominion serves, so not Dominion's own event records. Events are ranked by the highest count held for an hour; the peak is the highest fifteen-minute count. Hours to clear 90 percent run from the peak until 90 percent of the outage above the pre-event level has cleared. It's a net count, so it is not a utility's share of affected customers restored, which counts everyone affected and looks slower. Michael in 2018 is the largest, 558,729 customers out at the peak. The two winter storms took about 100 hours to clear 90 percent. Across 47 events with at least 50,000 customers out, after allowing for size, there's no evidence that clearing got faster: hours to clear 90 percent moved plus 4.6 percent a year, p = 0.07, not significant. Isaias, highlighted, is the largest event with a matching federal report: DOE-417 lists 508,000 customers affected in Virginia. The February 2025 event merges two storms.
