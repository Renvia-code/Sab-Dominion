Finding 1

Outage minutes rose from 130 to 133 because interruptions became more frequent, not longer

SAIDI, the average outage minutes per customer, is SAIFI (how often a customer loses power) times CAIDI (how long each interruption lasts). Dominion's 2025 Annual Report gives a 3-year SAIDI, excluding major events, of 133 minutes for 2023–25, up from 130 for 2022–24, and explains it (p. 10): “This increase is primarily due to increased storm activity.” The public Virginia data reproduces both reported figures to the minute: 130.2 and 133.0.

A 3-year average moves only when a year leaves the window: 2025 (144.4 minutes) replaced 2022 (136.0). Between those two years SAIFI rose 10% and CAIDI fell 4%, so the whole rise is frequency. That is consistent with Dominion's explanation: storm county-days were higher in 2025 than in 2022 (791 against 718; Finding 3). 2025's SAIFI, 1.320, is the highest in EIA's 2013–2025 record (the next highest is 1.303 in 2018).

Source: EIA-861 2025 final and PUDL (EIA-861) 2013–2024, Dominion Energy Virginia (utility 19876), excluding major event days. Reported figures: Dominion Energy 2025 Annual Report, p. 10.

Finding 2

Among 12 peer utilities, Dominion restores at a mid-pack pace; frequency is the gap

Twelve large investor-owned utilities in PJM and the Southeast, selected by EIA ID before results were computed. Averages of 2021–2025, excluding major event days. Dominion's SAIFI of 1.21 is the 4th highest of 12 (peer median 0.96); its CAIDI of 110 minutes is 6th of 12 (median 105). Its nearest neighbours are Duke Energy Progress and Georgia Power. On 3-year SAIDI (2023–25) Dominion is also 4th of 12, at 133.0 minutes.

Source: EIA-861 / PUDL 2021–2025, excluding major event days; means of annual values. The full table is in Appendix B.

The caveat to raise yourself: utilities set aside major event days differently. Most of these peers use the IEEE 1366 method; Dominion reports under its own rule, so the ranking is indicative, not exact. The pattern still matches Finding 1: the gap is how often customers lose power, not how fast it comes back.

Finding 3

County-days with a storm report had about 12 times the outage customer-hours of calm days

EAGLE-I, the Department of Energy's county outage record built from utilities' outage maps, gives the customers out every 15 minutes in each county. On the 6,108 county-days from 2015 to 2025 with a NOAA wind, winter or tropical storm report in a county Dominion serves, outage customer-hours per 1,000 customers were 11.9 times those of calm county-days (95% CI 7.9–16.9). It holds on non-major days (9.9×), in Dominion-only counties (19.9×) and in every year. It is an association: storm reports are partly triggered by damage. The map shows where ordinary-day outage minutes are highest: among counties with 20,000 or more customers, from 55 a year (Prince William County) to 633 (Pittsylvania County).

Source: EAGLE-I (ORNL for DOE), customers of all utilities in 99 of the 103 counties where EIA-861 2025 lists Dominion Energy Virginia (four cities left off: unusable data); NOAA Storm Events. Map: outage minutes per customer a year on non-major days, 2015–2024 average. Inset: Isaias, share of the excess outages restored, by hours after the peak.

Two more things to know

  • Year by year, storm county-days and SAIDI excluding major events move together: r = 0.70 over 2015–2024 (n = 10, p = 0.025; 95% CI 0.12 to 0.92). There were 791 storm county-days in 2025, up from 464 in 2024; a fit made without 2025 gives 145.3 minutes, against 144.4 actual. Adjusted for storm days, there is no clear trend since 2015 (+0.34 minutes a year, 95% range −1.41 to +2.08, p = 0.67).
  • Restoration: across 47 events with 50,000 or more customers out, there is no evidence it got faster (hours to 90% restored +4.6% a year after allowing for size, p = 0.07). Isaias: 351,738 customers out at the peak (EAGLE-I, all utilities in Dominion's counties), and 90% restored 33.5 hours after the peak.
What it cannot say: which circuits, which causes, or whether hardened lines held up. EAGLE-I counts every utility in a county, so it shows where and when, never Dominion's own SAIDI.

Finding 4

Overnight demand grew faster than afternoon demand, and winter rivals summer for the yearly peak

EIA-930 hourly demand for the PJM Dominion (DOM) zone, which is wider than Dominion's retail territory. From 2019 to 2025, average load in the hours ending 1–5 a.m. rose 39–41%, against about 27% in the hours ending 1–5 p.m. Weather does not explain it: holding Virginia's daily heating and cooling degree days and the weekday fixed, average load rose 33% (32% unadjusted). The highest hour of the year rose 26% (19,803 → 24,957 MW) and the lowest 52% (7,167 → 10,903 MW). Winter set the yearly peak in 2019, 2022 and 2025; 2026, to 29 October, is a tie (25,413 MW winter vs 25,384 MW summer).

Source: EIA-930 hourly demand, PJM DOM zone; *2026 to the hour ending midnight, 29 October (winter = January–February only). Winter = December–February, summer = June–August. Weather adjustment: daily load on Virginia degree days and weekday, 2019–2025 (exploratory).

  • Of the 50 highest hours in the EIA-930 record from July 2018 to December 2025, 47 fell in 2025.
  • Commercial electricity sales in Dominion's Virginia territory rose 65% from 2013 to 2025 (39.08 → 64.62 TWh) on 8% more commercial customers (EIA-861).
Don't say “data centers caused it”. Growth around the clock is consistent with large constant loads, but these data cannot identify customers. Say: “less overnight slack, and winter readiness matters as much as summer.”

Grid Resiliency, from Dominion's own description

  • Four programmes: mainfeeder hardening, voltage island mitigation, voltage optimisation, and intelligent grid devices (fault location, isolation and restoration). The Strategic Underground Program is separate.
  • Mainfeeder hardening selects feeders with at least 500 customers whose average SAIDI is more than 2 minutes worse than the rest, and with above-average outages over the last five years.
  • Dominion reports a 32% reduction in outage minutes on feeders hardened for at least 12 months (as of 30 November 2023).

How it's built

Built like governed production reporting, and the numbers to know

Public files land in Snowflake as raw text exactly as published (RAW), are typed and cleaned (STG), and become facts, dimensions and views (MART) that Power BI imports. Twelve tests run after each load (T1–T12, all pass). A data dictionary gives every column and measure a business definition, and a decisions log records 22 choices (D1–D22), such as which storm types count and which peers to use; the peers were fixed before any results were computed. The first check was reconciliation: 130.2 and 133.0 rebuilt against 130 and 133 reported; in all, 12 of 12 rebuilt values match their published figures. The county outage, storm and load tables add tests T13–T31 (all pass).

Appendix A

Definitions, and what public data cannot show

TermMeaning
SAIDIOutage minutes per customer per year. SAIDI = SAIFI × CAIDI. Dominion 2025: 1.320 × 109.4 = 144.4 minutes.
SAIFIInterruptions per customer per year: how often.
CAIDIMinutes per interruption: how long.
Major event daysDays set aside so one extreme storm does not swamp the trend. IEEE 1366 marks a day when its SAIDI exceeds a threshold set from the previous five years of daily SAIDI (the “2.5 beta” method). Dominion reports under its own rule.
ex-MEDExcluding major event days: the headline series in this brief.
CEMICustomers experiencing multiple interruptions: a count from outage records.
Log splitln(SAIDI ratio) = ln(SAIFI ratio) + ln(CAIDI ratio); the frequency share is ln(SAIFI ratio) ÷ ln(SAIDI ratio). 2023 → 2025: 0.125 ÷ 0.158 = 79%.
